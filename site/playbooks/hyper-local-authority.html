MBRACE Insight playbook

Your technicians know which neighborhoods have plaster walls and no ductwork, where the panels are undersized, and why the second floor runs hot in a 1960s split-level. Your website probably says "HVAC services in Bethesda, Potomac and Chevy Chase."

Hyper-local content turns what your team already knows into pages that answer specific homeowner questions better than anyone else in the area.

Why generic city pages fail

"HVAC Repair in [City]" pages say the same thing as every other contractor's page. Swapping the city name across twenty copies of the same template doesn't help either. Search engines treat that as low-value, mass-produced content.

The ruleEvery page has to contain something only your company could write: your jobs, your photos, your technicians' explanations and your honest pricing approach.

What each page targets

  1. A neighborhood or town you actually serve
  2. A housing type common there (1940s colonials, split-levels, row homes, large newer homes)
  3. A specific problem (no ductwork, uneven floors, humidity, an undersized panel, a boiler to replace)
Example titles, for a company that does this work
  • "Cooling a 1940s Bethesda Colonial Without Ductwork: Ductless and Small-Duct Options"
  • "Replacing a Boiler With a Heat Pump in an Older Takoma Park Home"
  • "Is Your Silver Spring Panel Ready for a Heat Pump? 100A vs. 200A Service"
Only publish a page for work your company actually does well.

Anatomy of a page

  1. The problem in plain language, and who has it
  2. Why it happens in this kind of house: construction era, ducts, insulation, electrical
  3. The real options, including when not to replace anything, and the trade-offs of each
  4. How your team handles it: measurement first (blower door, Manual J), then the installation steps
  5. A real job: before and after, written up with the homeowner's permission and identifying details removed
  6. Your pricing approach: what drives the cost, and ranges only if your company stands behind them
  7. Rebates: which programs might apply, with sources and the date you checked
  8. FAQ: the questions customers actually ask your team
  9. Your team's photos from real jobs in the area
  10. A clear next step

Length follows content. A thorough page often runs 1,500 to 2,500 words, but a shorter page with real detail beats a long page with filler.

Don't publish claims you can't back up

Leave these out unless they're true and documented
  • "24/7 availability," if nobody answers at 2am
  • "Background-checked technicians," unless you actually run checks
  • Special credentials or compliance for hospitals, government or medical clients
  • "Trusted by [group]" lines without real customers who'd say so
  • Case studies, reviews or numbers that didn't happen

How we build them

  1. Research: your top neighborhoods and housing types, the questions your customers ask, and what's already published nearby.
  2. Interview: 20 minutes with a technician or comfort advisor per topic. This is where the unique material comes from.
  3. Collect proof: real job photos and a permission-cleared job write-up.
  4. Write and review: every page is fact-checked against your team's answers and dated sources before it goes live.
  5. Technical setup: structured data (LocalBusiness, Service, FAQ), internal links, and links from your Google Business Profile posts.

How to measure it

Don't judge a page in its first month. After about 90 days, check for each page: impressions and clicks in Google Search Console, calls and forms from that page, and closed jobs attributed to it. Expand the topics that bring work, and fix or retire the ones that don't.

Action steps

  1. List your four best neighborhoods and the two most common house types in each.
  2. Ask your techs: "What's the question homeowners there always ask?"
  3. Pick three topics and build one page at a time, with real photos.
